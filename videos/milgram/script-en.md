# Milgram : script complet (anglais)

*Format : explication en psychologie animée, environ 9 à 10 minutes (environ 1 400 mots de narration). Langue : anglais. Visuels : motion design 2D/2.5D (Remotion), sans image d'archive protégée.*

---

## Titres (10 options)
1. Would You Have Pressed the 450-Volt Button?
2. The Experiment That Proved Good People Obey Terrible Orders
3. 65% of People Went All the Way. Would You?
4. Why Your Brain Obeys (Even When It Knows It's Wrong)
5. The Milgram Experiment: What Really Happened
6. The Psychology Experiment Yale Couldn't Forget
7. Why Normal People Do Terrible Things
8. "You Have No Other Choice, You Must Go On"
9. The Dark Truth About Obedience
10. You Think You'd Refuse. The Data Says Otherwise.

**À tester en priorité avec Test & Compare** : n°1 (le spectateur se projette), n°3 (un chiffre fort), n°10 (il conteste ce que le spectateur croit de lui-même).

## Miniatures (3 concepts)
- **A. Le bouton** : un gros interrupteur rouge « 450V » au premier plan, un doigt qui s'en approche, fond sombre. Texte : « WOULD YOU? »
- **B. Le chiffre** : « 65% » en très grand, en jaune, à côté d'un panneau d'interrupteurs dont le dernier porte la mention « XXX ». Pas d'autre texte.
- **C. Le dilemme** : un personnage stylisé, la main sur le levier ; derrière lui, une silhouette en blouse blanche, et un mur avec « DANGER ». Texte : « OBEY? »

## Script

> **[NARRATION]** est le texte lu par la voix off. **[VISUEL]** donne les indications pour l'animation.

### 0:00 – 0:45 · Accroche
**[VISUEL]** Écran noir. Un néon s'allume : un panneau métallique avec 30 interrupteurs alignés, de 15 V à 450 V. Les étiquettes s'allument une à une : *Slight Shock… Moderate… Strong… Danger: Severe Shock… XXX*.

**[NARRATION]**
It's 1961. You answer a newspaper ad: four dollars and fifty cents to take part in a memory study at Yale University.
You sit down in front of a machine with thirty switches. The first one says *fifteen volts*. The last one just says… *XXX*.
In the next room, a man is strapped to a chair. Every time he gets an answer wrong, you're told to press the next switch.
Most people are sure they'd stop early. Maybe at the first scream.
But when this experiment was actually run, sixty-five percent of people went all the way to the end.
So the real question isn't *what would you do?*
It's: *why do almost all of us overestimate how brave we'd be?*

**[VISUEL]** Le chiffre « 65% » tombe au centre et fait trembler l'écran. Titre de la vidéo.

### 0:45 – 2:00 · Le dispositif
**[VISUEL]** Plan en coupe du laboratoire, comme une maison de poupée : trois pièces, trois personnages (le Professeur, l'« Élève », le Sujet). Des flèches montrent qui voit qui.

**[NARRATION]**
Here's how it worked. Two volunteers arrive. They draw slips of paper to decide who'll be the *teacher* and who'll be the *learner*.
But the draw is rigged. The learner is an actor, a friendly middle-aged man who mentions, almost casually, that he has a slight heart condition.
The real subject is you, the teacher.
You read pairs of words. He has to remember them. Every mistake: one more switch, fifteen volts higher.
The shocks are fake. The machine is fake. But *you* don't know that.
And standing behind you is a man in a grey lab coat. Calm. Polite. He's the experimenter. And he has four sentences prepared.

**[VISUEL]** Les quatre phrases apparaissent une à une, de plus en plus grandes :
*Please continue. / The experiment requires that you continue. / It is absolutely essential that you continue. / You have no other choice. You must go on.*

### 2:00 – 3:45 · L'escalade
**[VISUEL]** Une jauge de tension monte. À chaque palier, une bulle de dialogue venant du mur (texte seulement, pas de son violent).

**[NARRATION]**
At first, it's easy. Fifteen volts. Thirty. Forty-five.
At seventy-five volts, the learner grunts.
At one hundred and fifty, he shouts that he wants out. That his heart is bothering him.
This is the moment almost everyone turns around and looks at the man in the lab coat.
*Please continue.*
And here's the trick: no single step is a big decision. You're never asked to give someone 450 volts. You're only ever asked to go *fifteen volts further than last time*.
Psychologists call this incremental commitment. Once you've pressed the 150-volt switch, refusing at 165 means admitting the last ten switches were wrong too.
At three hundred volts, the learner pounds on the wall.
After three hundred and thirty… silence.
And the experimenter says: *Treat no answer as a wrong answer.*

**[VISUEL]** Silence total pendant 2 secondes. Le mur reste immobile. La jauge continue de monter, seule.

### 3:45 – 5:00 · Le résultat
**[VISUEL]** Quarante petites silhouettes alignées. Elles s'arrêtent à différents paliers ; 26 d'entre elles avancent jusqu'au bout et deviennent rouges.

**[NARRATION]**
Before the study, Milgram asked psychiatrists to predict the results. They guessed that only a tiny fraction, around one in a thousand, would go all the way.
In this version of the experiment, twenty-six out of forty did.
These weren't monsters. They were teachers, salesmen, postal workers. Many were sweating, trembling, laughing nervously. Some begged to stop.
And then they pressed the next switch.

### 5:00 – 6:45 · Pourquoi le cerveau obéit
**[VISUEL]** Un cerveau stylisé. Trois zones s'allument tour à tour, chacune avec une icône : 🎓 (autorité), 🪜 (escalier), 🎒 (sac transféré à quelqu'un d'autre).

**[NARRATION]**
So what was going on in their heads? Three things.
**One: authority.** We are trained from childhood to defer to experts. A lab coat, a university, a calm voice: your brain reads all of it as *this person knows something I don't*.
**Two: small steps.** Nobody decides to do something terrible. They decide to do something *slightly worse than what they've already done*.
**Three: the agentic state.** That's Milgram's own term. People stop seeing themselves as the author of their actions, and start seeing themselves as an instrument of someone else's will. Several participants asked: *Who's responsible if something happens to him?* And when the experimenter said *I am*, many of them kept going.
Responsibility didn't disappear. It just moved, and that made it bearable.

### 6:45 – 8:00 · Ce qui fait chuter l'obéissance
**[VISUEL]** Un tableau de bord avec plusieurs curseurs. Chaque variante fait baisser une barre de pourcentage.

**[NARRATION]**
Here's the hopeful part. Milgram ran many variations, and obedience wasn't fixed. It moved.
When the experimenter gave orders by phone instead of in person, obedience dropped sharply.
When the teacher had to physically press the learner's hand onto a plate, it dropped again.
And when two other "teachers", also actors, refused to continue, almost everyone else refused too.
That's the most important finding of all: one person saying *no* makes it dramatically easier for everyone else to say no.

### 8:00 – 9:00 · La nuance
**[VISUEL]** Une loupe sur un dossier d'archives stylisé : des notes, des points d'interrogation.

**[NARRATION]**
To be fair, the experiment has been heavily criticized. Researchers who later went through Milgram's archives found that some participants suspected the shocks weren't real, and that the experimenter sometimes went beyond the script.
And today, a study like this would never pass an ethics board.
But the core finding has held up. In a partial replication in 2009, psychologist Jerry Burger stopped at 150 volts, for ethical reasons. Even so, most participants were still willing to continue past that point.

### 9:00 – 9:45 · Conclusion
**[VISUEL]** Retour au panneau d'interrupteurs. Un seul interrupteur reste éteint : il porte l'étiquette *NO*.

**[NARRATION]**
So, would you have pressed the 450-volt switch?
The honest answer is: you don't know. Nobody does, until they're in the room.
But now you know how the trap works. Authority. Small steps. Handing off responsibility.
And you know the most powerful thing you can do when you see it happening:
be the first person to say *no*.

**[VISUEL]** L'interrupteur « NO » s'allume en vert. Fin.

## Faits utilisés (à revérifier avant publication)
- 1961, Université Yale ; annonce dans un journal ; 4,50 $ par participant.
- Générateur de 15 V à 450 V, par paliers de 15 V (30 interrupteurs), étiquettes de *Slight Shock* à *Danger: Severe Shock*, puis *XXX*.
- Les quatre relances de l'expérimentateur (*Please continue…*, *You have no other choice, you must go on*).
- Version de référence : 26 sujets sur 40 (65 %) vont jusqu'à 450 V.
- Prédiction des psychiatres : environ 1 sur 1 000 irait jusqu'au bout.
- Variantes : ordres par téléphone, contact physique avec l'élève, deux pairs qui refusent : l'obéissance baisse fortement.
- « État agentique » : terme de Milgram (*Obedience to Authority*, 1974).
- Critiques : Gina Perry (*Behind the Shock Machine*, 2012) ; réplication partielle de Jerry Burger (2009), arrêtée à 150 V.
